Demos / Virtual kiosk test

Ordering a meal, as a cognitive test

Using a kiosk asks more of you than it looks: hold an order in memory, find each item on an unfamiliar menu, and act. In our JMIR study, how people did exactly that separated participants with mild cognitive impairment from healthy controls. This is that task, in a browser — the order is given once, and the kiosk does not remind you of it. It takes about a minute, and what happens to your numbers afterwards is the subject of a second study.

The order is played once and never repeated. Memorise it, then complete it yourself on the kiosk below.

What this measures

The study drew four biomarkers from this task. Three of them survive the move to a browser, because a mouse or a fingertip stands in for the hand controller well enough:

  • Time to completion — how long the six steps take.
  • Number of errors — selections that were not the item asked for.
  • Hand movement speed — distance travelled, divided by time. Mouse only: a dragged mouse traces a path, a fingertip only reports where it landed, so on a touchscreen this one is left blank.

The fourth needed the eye tracker built into the headset — scanpath length, how far the eyes travelled. Its absence here is the clearest answer to why the study used VR rather than a screen.

In the study, participants with mild cognitive impairment were slower, made more errors and had longer scanpaths than healthy controls. Combined with MRI biomarkers, this detected impairment with 94.4% accuracy.
