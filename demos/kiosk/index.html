Demos / Virtual kiosk test

Ordering a meal, as a cognitive test

Using a kiosk asks more of you than it looks: hold an order in memory, find each item on an unfamiliar menu, and act. In our JMIR study, how people did exactly that separated participants with mild cognitive impairment from healthy controls. This is that task, in a browser — the order is given once, and the kiosk does not remind you of it.

The screens are the Korean originals from the study; the task is spoken once in English before you start, and not repeated.

What this measures

The study drew four biomarkers from the virtual kiosk test. Three of them survive the move to a browser, because a mouse or a fingertip stands in for the hand controller well enough:

  • Time to completion — how long all six steps take. Measured here exactly as in the study.
  • Number of errors — selections that were not the item asked for. Measured here exactly as in the study.
  • Hand movement speed — total distance the hand travelled, divided by time. Here it is pointer distance instead of a controller tracked by base stations.
  • Scanpath length — how far the eyes travelled. Not reproduced. This one needed the eye tracker built into the headset, and it is the clearest answer to why the study used VR rather than a screen.

In the study, participants with mild cognitive impairment moved their hands more slowly, took longer, made more errors, and had longer scanpaths than healthy controls. Combining these behavioural measures with MRI biomarkers detected impairment with 94.4% accuracy.
