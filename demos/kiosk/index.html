Demos / Virtual kiosk test

Ordering a meal, as a cognitive test

Using a kiosk asks more of you than it looks: hold an order in memory, find each item on an unfamiliar menu, and act. In our JMIR study, how people did exactly that separated participants with mild cognitive impairment from healthy controls. This is that task, in a browser — told once, no reminders, about a minute. Then a second study writes your result up two ways.

A demonstration, not a screening test — nothing here can diagnose anyone. Your time and errors never leave the browser; the only things that leave are two anonymous counts — a run started, a run finished.

The task

What this measures

The study drew four biomarkers from this task. Two survive the move to a browser:

  • Time to completion — how long the whole order takes.
  • Number of errors — selections that were not the item asked for.

The other two stay with the study’s hardware. Hand movement speed came from a tracked hand controller, in meters per second, which a mouse in pixels cannot stand in for; scanpath length needed the eye tracker built into the headset, and it was there that the impaired group’s eyes travelled furthest — the clearest answer to why the study used VR rather than a screen.

In the study the impaired group was slower and made more errors. With MRI biomarkers added, the study’s model told the two groups apart with 94.4% accuracy — a number that belongs to that full study, not to anything this page measures.
