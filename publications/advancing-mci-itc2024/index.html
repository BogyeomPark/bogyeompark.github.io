Advancing Mild Cognitive Impairment Detection: Integrating VR, MRI, and Neuropsychological Insights for Comprehensive Diagnosis

Bogyeom Park, Jinseok Park, Hojin Choi, Hokyoung Ryu, and Kyoungwon Seo

ITC-CSCC 2024 · Pages 1–6 · Published July 2024

Open PDF ↗Publisher ↗
On this pageOverviewMethodKey FindingsContributionCitation

Overview

Diagnosing mild cognitive impairment has relied on MRI and neuropsychological testing, both of which are slow and costly. Virtual reality promises behavioral evidence from an ordinary task instead, but whether it belongs beside the established measures or merely near them was unsettled. This study asks how VR biomarkers relate to the conventional ones, and whether adding them changes what a diagnosis can see.

Method

Participants completed a virtual kiosk task while hand movement, eye movement, and task performance were recorded, alongside structural MRI and neuropsychological assessment. Support vector machine models were trained on each modality alone and on their combinations, and compared on accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 and AUC.

Key Findings

94.4%Accuracy when VR biomarkers joined the neuropsychological measures, with 93.8% sensitivity and 94.4% specificity.
87.0%Accuracy of VR biomarkers on their own, close behind the 90.7% neuropsychological benchmark and ahead of MRI at 83.3%.
95.2%F1 score of the combined model, at 96.8% precision.

Contribution

  • Behavioral evidence from a fifteen-minute everyday task nearly matches a full neuropsychological battery.
  • Fixation duration correlated with atrophy in the hippocampus (r = .34, p = .013), entorhinal cortex (r = .28, p = .039) and amygdala (r = .28, p = .044), linking what a person does to what the scan shows.
  • Adding VR to conventional biomarkers raises accuracy rather than replacing the tests clinicians already trust.
